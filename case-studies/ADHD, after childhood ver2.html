Research case study

ADHD, after childhood

How a condition that starts in childhood keeps shaping adult life, and why so many people end up leaning on a tool to hold their day together.

November 2026 Asawari Sakharakar | Design for Social Impact

01 — Childhood

It starts early. It is often missed.

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition, not a character flaw. It affects attention, impulse control, memory and emotional regulation. To meet diagnostic criteria, symptoms must be persistent, rooted in childhood and disruptive in at least two settings: work, school or home.

And yet many people grow up without a diagnosis. Four things keep it hidden.

BARRIER 01

Access

Diagnosis depends on someone noticing and being able to act. Caregivers act as gatekeepers, and diagnosis rates vary by race and community.

BARRIER 02

Not knowing the symptoms

The signs get read as personality rather than as a condition, so nobody thinks to look further.

BARRIER 03

Masking

Mirroring other people’s behaviour in social settings so that nothing looks out of place.

BARRIER 04

Dysfunctional coping

Strategies for avoiding anxiety-heavy situations. They manage the moment, not the condition.

Child with ADHDUndiagnosed adult

Why it is hard to diagnose, even when someone looks

ADHD symptoms overlap with other conditions, so they are easily confused with each other:

Depressive disorders Anxiety disorders Antisocial personality disorder Borderline personality disorder PTSD & childhood trauma

ADHD alongside borderline personality disorder is described as the biggest diagnostic challenge.

Trauma is the quietest look-alike. Hypervigilance can read as inattention, and intrusive memories as distractibility. Up to 17% of trauma-exposed children meet ADHD criteria, and adults with ADHD are nearly seven times more likely to have PTSD. So a missed trauma history can hide behind an ADHD label, and the reverse.

Source: PTSD UK; Szymanski et al. (2011); Boodoo et al.

02 — Adulthood

It doesn’t end at eighteen. It gets more complicated.

Symptoms change as people age. Left unnamed, the coping habits of childhood carry on, and they stop working once life asks for more.

Childhood

Traits are there, but unread

Inattention, impulsivity and big feelings are seen by others as temperament. Coping strategies begin here.

Teens

Feeling different

Emotional problems tend to grow with age, often from peer rejection, constant friction with teachers, and the sense of being different. Planning skills are still maturing, and at home a parent often does the planning, which quietly covers the gap.

Higher study & work

The pressure to present a certain way

Responsibility rises, and so does the need to show up in an expected manner. The old coping mechanisms for emotions start to catch up.

Late 20s

Holding life together

When the person who planned is no longer there, deadlines start to slip. Work they love can pull them into tunnel vision and time blindness; work they don’t love is hard to stay in, so many struggle to hold a job.

Adult life

Depression and generalised anxiety

What began as an unseen difference turns into more complicated problems, such as depression and generalised anxiety disorder (GAD).

16–31%

of adults with ADHD currently have major depression, with lifetime rates as high as 45%.

25–43%

of adults with ADHD meet criteria for generalised anxiety disorder, with lifetime rates as high as 59%.

Most people cope. They build their own systems to hold back the drawbacks: overstimulation, emotional frustration and impulsivity.

But there is one thing a personal system can’t reach.

03 — The gap

Planning, schedules and deadlines.

Most people do these almost without thinking. For many with ADHD, these are exactly the things that slip, even when they badly want them not to.

PlanningKnowing where to begin and what comes next
SchedulingHolding time in mind and sequencing it
Keeping deadlinesFollowing through on time, every time

These skills keep maturing through the teens. Brain-imaging studies suggest that in ADHD this development can follow a different, later timeline (Yasumura et al., 2019; Hoogman et al., 2017). It is a difference in development, not in effort.

What runs the system instead: dopamine

Illustration of the idea, not measured data.

ADHD brains tend to have lower baseline dopamine activity, so they are pulled toward instant reward and novelty. When dopamine is dysregulated, effortful tasks stay out of reach and frustration follows.

Screens can turn time distortion into hours lost, and hyperfocus into compulsion.

A new text can hit the brain’s reward system and make the next effortful task feel even duller.

Even with tools, the same walls remain

In one study, participants had already tried many productivity and support tools and still struggled with:

04 — Real life

From the outside, it looks like character.

When executive function fails in public, people don’t see a condition. They see a person, and they attach labels. These are the ones that came up in interviews.

extremely moody forgetful undisciplined careless depressive wasted potential unreliable

Being close doesn’t mean understanding

The interviews point to a pattern: the people nearest to someone with ADHD tend to reach for personality explanations, not neurodevelopmental ones.

Many try to fix it as a personal responsibility. But that is a heavy load to carry while working and pursuing higher studies, and it is exactly where the weight lands.

05 — The weight

In India, there is no clear route to ask

ADHD is not among the 21 disability categories recognised under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016. In workplace research, people mostly relied on their own strategies, such as limiting distractions, scheduling tasks and taking breaks.

So the people most affected go looking for a solution, or lean heavily on a tool to stand in for their executive function.

06 — The tool

The tool becomes part of the problem.

For many adults with ADHD, the phone is an external brain: reminders, calendars, notes, messages. But the phone is also built around interruption and endless content.

  1. The phone runs the day.Reminders, calendar, notes and communication all live in one place.
  2. It needs executive function to use.Starting, sequencing and returning are exactly what is hardest.
  3. It also interrupts.Constant pings, endless content, and tasks left half-finished.
  4. The person blames themselvesfor “not using it properly”, and tries again.

Tools for body doubling, time blocking, reminders and gamified tasks do exist (Todoist, Notion, Trello, Forest, Focusmate, Google Keep). But each solves one problem, and none are connected to each other, so results are hit or miss.

The system is another job

So people build their own: physical cues that remind them to do things in order. The even bigger task is creating that system, finding what actually works, then testing a tool and fitting it into daily life. That effort is itself an executive-function task, which is why adoption so often fails.

Source: primary interviews; Chalmers University study.

The question

How might we design a system that supports a missing executive function, and makes life easier?

The aim is to cover the executive-function gap quietly, building on patterns people have already learned, using the existing tools they already rely on or find work better for them. Those patterns come from user research, validated by experts and research papers.

The proposal is a system that works like their brain and for their brain: it plans, executes, and gives a physical cue when something needs doing, helping point tunnel vision at the task at hand. It needs no diagnosis, no disclosure and no labels.
